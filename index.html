Choosing a UX Framework

How to teach the three problem types (need, usability and engagement) so students learn to diagnose a problem before picking a principle. Each framework comes with an industry example and, where it helps, a mockup.

DPM Cohort, IIM Visakhapatnam About 25 minutes Facilitator: Rajdip Sengupta

Industry examples are well-known public case studies, simplified for teaching. Mockups are generic illustrations, not screenshots of the products named. Check specific figures before quoting them in class.

01

Learning goals

By the end of this segment, students should be able to:

  1. Turn a vague complaint ("the feature isn't working") into a user-behaviour symptom.
  2. Classify that symptom as a need, usability or engagement problem.
  3. Name two or three frameworks that suit each type, and explain why.
  4. Choose the right prototype fidelity and success metric for the problem type.

03

The core idea

"Don't start with a principle. Start with the symptom, diagnose the type of problem, then pick the lens."

Teams often reach for a favourite tool (say, "let's gamify it") before they know what's actually wrong. Every UX framework answers a different question, and using one on the wrong problem wastes the prototype, the sprint and the user's patience. The five steps are: observe the symptom, diagnose the problem type, pick the UX principle, prototype at the right fidelity, then measure and iterate.

Diagnose the problem type
Pick the UX principle
Prototype at the right fidelity
Measure and iterate
Teaching tip: Ask the three diagnostic questions in order. If the answer to the first is "no", stop there; there's no point fixing usability for a product nobody needs.
  1. Need: Do users actually want this? Are we solving the right problem?
  2. Usability: If they want it, can they complete the task easily?
  3. Engagement: If they can, do they come back?
04 · Problem type 1

Need problem

"We aren't sure what users really want."

What it is

A need problem means the product or feature doesn't match a real job the user is trying to get done. The interface may be perfectly usable, but the value isn't there. This is the most expensive problem to discover late, because it means the wrong thing was built.

Signals in the data

  • Low adoption even among users who saw or tried the feature
  • Users find workarounds (Excel, WhatsApp) instead of the feature
  • Feature requests that contradict each other
  • Training and announcements don't move adoption; nobody uses it

What users say

  • "Nice, but I don't really need it."
  • "I already do this another way."
  • "Can it also do X?" (X being something entirely different)

Frameworks to use

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

Jobs-to-be-Done says users "hire" a product to make progress in a specific situation. Focus on the job, not on features or demographics. The competition is anything else the user could hire for the same job.

How to apply: Write a job statement: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." Interview users about the last time they did the job, and what they "fired" in order to hire your product.

In industry · The milkshake study

Clayton Christensen's team helped a fast-food chain that wanted to sell more milkshakes. Surveys on flavour and price didn't move sales. Watching buyers did: a large share of shakes were bought early in the morning by commuters driving alone. They "hired" the shake to make a long, boring drive more interesting and stay full until lunch. Its real competitors were bananas, bagels and boredom, not other shakes. The fix was a thicker shake that lasts the whole commute and a faster way to buy it.

Classroom bridge: A B2B procurement tool built a rich catalogue, but buyers' real job was "get purchases approved before quarter-end without chasing managers." The answer was an approval workflow, not a better catalogue.

Design Thinking (Empathise → Define)

Design Thinking has five stages: Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. For a need problem, the first two matter most: understand users deeply, then state the problem sharply before generating ideas.

How to apply: Observe users in context (shadowing, interviews), build an empathy map of what they say, think, do and feel, then write a "How might we…" problem statement.

In industry · GE Healthcare's MRI "Adventure Series"

Doug Dietz, a GE designer, saw a frightened child crying on her way into an MRI scanner he had designed. Many young children needed sedation to stay still for scans. Instead of re-engineering the machine, the team spent time with children and hospital staff and reframed the problem: "How might we make the scan feel like an adventure?" Scan rooms were redesigned as pirate ships and jungle trips, with scripts for staff. Hospitals reported far fewer children needing sedation.

Also: In Airbnb's early days, the founders visited New York hosts and noticed most listings had poor photos. They took professional photos themselves, and bookings in the city rose sharply. That insight came from empathy work, not from data.

User journey mapping

A user journey map shows every stage of the user's experience toward a goal: actions, thoughts, emotions and touchpoints, including the parts outside your product.

How to apply: Draw the stages left to right and plot the emotion curve. The lowest points are where the unmet need sits, and often they aren't on any screen you own.

In industry · Disney's MagicBand

Mapping a theme-park visit end to end showed that the pain wasn't the rides. It was the friction between them: tickets at the gate, room keys at the hotel, wallets at every shop, long queues. Disney's answer was the MagicBand, one wristband that works as park ticket, hotel room key, payment method and ride-reservation pass. The low points on the map became the product.

Classroom bridge: Map a home-loan journey and the biggest pain point is collecting documents across several offices, not the app. The opportunity is document pickup, not a nicer form.

Prototype fidelity

For a need problem use low fidelity: paper sketches, storyboards, low-fi wireframes, even a fake landing page ("fake door") or a "concierge" test where you do the job manually for a few users. You're testing the value, so polish distracts.

Metrics to watch

  • Interest or intent: sign-ups on a fake-door test, willingness to pay
  • Problem validation: how many interviewed users describe the same pain unprompted
  • Adoption rate among the target segment once launched
Say this: "If users don't need it, no amount of good design will save it. Validate the job before you design the screens."
05 · Problem type 2

Usability problem

"Users drop off, get confused or make errors."

What it is

A usability problem means users want the outcome, but the interface gets in the way. The need is real; the path to it is too long, unclear or error-prone. This is the most common problem type and usually the cheapest to fix.

Signals in the data

  • Sharp drop-off or abandonment at a specific step in the funnel
  • High error rates, form re-submissions, "back" taps
  • Long time-on-task for simple jobs
  • Support tickets that ask "how do I…?"

What users say

  • "I couldn't find where to…"
  • "I wasn't sure if it went through."
  • "I tried three times and gave up."

Frameworks to use

Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics

Nielsen's 10 heuristics are rules of thumb from Jakob Nielsen for checking a design. The ones students will use most:

  • Visibility of system status: always show what's happening (progress bars, "Payment processing…").
  • Match with the real world: use the user's words, not internal jargon ("Order ID", not "SO Number").
  • User control and freedom: easy undo and cancel.
  • Error prevention: design so mistakes can't happen (disable invalid dates, confirm big transfers).
  • Recognition over recall: show options instead of making users remember (recent searches, pre-filled values).

How to apply: Run a heuristic evaluation: 3–5 evaluators each walk the flow and log violations by heuristic and severity.

In industry
  • Visibility of system status: food-delivery and ride-hailing apps show live order and driver tracking ("Preparing → Picked up → 6 min away"), which cuts "where is my order?" calls.
  • User control and freedom: Gmail's "Undo Send" gives a short window to take an email back.
  • Error prevention: Gmail warns you if your email mentions an attachment but none is attached. Banking apps fetch the bank and branch from the IFSC code before you can submit.
  • Recognition over recall: search boxes suggest queries and show recent searches; payment apps list saved payees so nobody retypes account numbers.

Hick's Law

Hick's Law says the time to make a decision grows with the number and complexity of choices. Too many options cause hesitation, then abandonment.

How to apply: Cut options, group them, set a smart default, or reveal choices progressively.

In industry · Google vs the web portals

In the early 2000s, portal homepages packed in news, weather, stocks, mail and dozens of links. Google's homepage offered essentially one choice: a search box. Fewer decisions meant users got to the task immediately, and the minimal homepage became a signature of the product.

Checkout version: UPI and food-delivery apps put the last-used payment method and a couple of popular options first, with everything else behind "More options", instead of listing 15 methods at once.

Fitts's Law and visual hierarchy

Fitts's Law: the time to reach a target depends on its size and distance. Big, close targets are faster and cause fewer mis-taps. Visual hierarchy: size, contrast, colour and position tell the eye what to look at first.

How to apply: Make the primary action large and within thumb reach (the bottom of the screen on mobile). Give it the strongest contrast, and make secondary actions visibly quieter.

In industry
  • Thumb reach: with iOS 15, Apple moved Safari's address bar to the bottom of the screen by default, within easy reach of the thumb on large phones.
  • Sticky primary action: food-delivery and e-commerce apps keep a full-width "Place order" or "Add to cart" bar fixed at the bottom, so the main action is always the biggest, closest target.
  • Visual hierarchy: on Amazon's product page, the price and the brightly coloured "Add to Cart" and "Buy Now" buttons stand out against an otherwise neutral page.

Prototype fidelity

For a usability problem use medium fidelity: a clickable Figma prototype with realistic layout and copy. Users need to actually try the flow, but visual polish isn't the point yet.

Metrics to watch

  • Task success rate and time-on-task in usability tests
  • Step-by-step funnel conversion
  • Error rate; "how do I" support tickets
  • System Usability Scale (SUS) score
Worked example from the slide: Bank-statement download has high drop-off. It's a usability problem, because users clearly want the statement. Apply error prevention (block impossible date ranges) and recognition over recall (one-tap "Last 3 months", "Last FY"). Test with a clickable prototype, and measure completion rate and time-to-download.
06 · Problem type 3

Engagement problem

"Users try it once and never come back."

What it is

An engagement problem means users need the product and can use it, but it doesn't become part of their routine. First use goes fine; retention collapses. This is about behaviour and motivation over time, not a single screen.

Signals in the data

  • Good activation, but a steep Day-1 / Day-7 / Day-30 retention drop
  • Low DAU/MAU ratio (stickiness)
  • High uninstall or churn rate after the first week
  • Notifications ignored or turned off

What users say

  • "It was fine, I just forgot about it."
  • "I didn't see a reason to open it again."
  • "Too many notifications, so I turned them off."

Frameworks to use

Hook Model (Nir Eyal)

The Hook Model is a four-step loop that builds habits:

  1. Trigger: external (notification, email) at first, then internal (boredom, anxiety, "I wonder if…").
  2. Action: the simplest behaviour done in anticipation of a reward (open the app, scroll).
  3. Variable reward: something unpredictable and satisfying (new deals, likes, a surprise cashback).
  4. Investment: the user puts something in (a wishlist, streak, followers, saved preferences) that makes the next cycle more valuable and loads the next trigger.
In industry · Duolingo

Duolingo is a textbook hook. Trigger: a daily reminder from its mascot ("Time for your Spanish lesson!"). Action: a lesson short enough to finish in a few minutes. Variable reward: XP, league rankings and surprise achievements. Investment: the streak itself; the longer it gets, the more it hurts to lose it, which becomes the internal trigger for tomorrow.

Classroom bridge: A fitness app sends a 7 a.m. reminder (trigger), the user logs a 10-minute workout (action), sees a streak and a random badge (variable reward), and sets tomorrow's goal (investment).

Fogg Behaviour Model (B = MAP)

BJ Fogg's model says a Behaviour happens only when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt meet at the same moment. If the behaviour isn't happening, one of the three is missing. High motivation can make up for a hard task, and a very easy task needs little motivation; the prompt has to arrive when the person is above the "action line".

How to apply: Diagnose which element is weak. Low motivation: show the value or a reward. Low ability: make the action easier (often the quickest win). No prompt: add a timely, relevant trigger.

In industry · Raising ability

UPI scan-and-pay: paying a street vendor digitally was low-motivation (it's ₹30) and used to be low-ability (cards, PINs, card machines). Scan a QR and enter a PIN made it so easy that the behaviour spread even for tiny amounts. Amazon 1-Click did the same for online shopping: removing checkout steps raised ability, so purchases happened at lower motivation.

Classroom bridge: Users want to save (motivation) and get reminders (prompt), but setting up a SIP takes 12 steps (ability). Cutting it to a 2-tap "Start ₹500/month" lifts conversion more than any extra reminder.

Peak-End Rule (Daniel Kahneman)

The Peak-End Rule says people judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment (the peak) and how it ended, not by the average of every moment.

How to apply: Deliberately design one delightful high point and a strong finish. Fix any painful peak first, because a bad peak dominates the memory.

In industry
  • IKEA: the store is a long, one-way maze and you often assemble the furniture yourself, yet people remember it fondly. It's often cited for its peaks (room set-ups you can walk into) and its ending: cheap snacks and ice cream right after the checkout.
  • Mailchimp: sending an email campaign is stressful. Mailchimp used to end it with its mascot giving a celebratory high-five, turning the riskiest moment into the most memorable one.
  • Classroom bridge: a long insurance claim feels much better if it ends with an instant "Approved, ₹42,000 credited" screen and a thank-you, rather than a silent status change.

Prototype fidelity

For an engagement problem use high fidelity: a hi-fi or vibe-coded prototype with real or realistic data, ideally used over several days (a diary study). Habits can't be tested in one 20-minute session.

Metrics to watch

  • Retention curves (D1, D7, D30) and cohort analysis
  • DAU/MAU stickiness and session frequency
  • Notification opt-out and response rate
Ethics note for the class: Engagement frameworks can tip into dark patterns, such as fake countdown timers, guilt-trip copy or endless feeds. Good test: would the user thank you for this hook if they understood how it works? Use these frameworks to help users keep habits they actually want, and avoid manipulative design.

08

Class exercise: diagnose these

Read each scenario aloud. Give pairs 60 seconds to choose a problem type and one framework. Reveal the answers one at a time.

1. A food-delivery app: users add items to the cart but abandon at payment.

Usability They clearly want food. Check Hick's Law (too many payment options) and visibility of system status (payment stuck with no feedback).

2. A new fitness app: 70% of users uninstall within a week.

Engagement Onboarding worked; habit didn't. Apply the Hook Model: morning trigger, streaks as variable reward, goal-setting as investment.

3. A B2B procurement tool that nobody uses, even after training.

Need Training didn't help, which suggests it's not a usability issue. Use JTBD: buyers' real job is fast approvals, not browsing a catalogue.

4. A banking app: 40% of fund transfers fail because of wrong IFSC codes.

Usability Error prevention and recognition over recall: auto-fetch the IFSC from bank and branch, and validate before submit.

5. An edtech app: students finish the first lesson happily but rarely start lesson two.

Engagement Fogg: the prompt is missing (no reminder), and possibly ability (lesson two is 45 minutes). Also apply Peak-End: make lesson one end on a cliffhanger.

6. A grocery app launches "recipe videos". Views are tiny, and users say they use YouTube for that.

Need The job is already well served elsewhere. Run journey mapping or JTBD: maybe the real job is "buy all ingredients for tonight's dinner in one tap."

Debrief prompt: "Which clue gave it away?" Push students to cite evidence (what users do and what they say), not gut feel.
09

Misdiagnosis traps

Trap 1: treating every low number as usability. Low conversion can mean users don't want it (need). Check: do users who complete the flow come back and value it?
Trap 2: gamifying a need problem. Streaks and badges on a product with no real value only delay the drop-off.
Trap 3: building hi-fi to answer a need question. Polished prototypes get polished feedback ("change the colour") and hide the real question: "Would you use this?"
Trap 4: one type at a time. Real products often have layered problems. Fix in order: need, then usability, then engagement.
10

Likely student questions

"Can a problem be more than one type?"

Yes, often. Solve them in order: there's no point making an unwanted feature easier to use, or more habit-forming. Diagnose the earliest broken layer first.

"How do I tell need from usability when adoption is low?"

Watch a few users try it. If they complete the task easily but shrug, it's a need problem. If they want the outcome but struggle, it's usability. Five usability tests usually settle it.

"Aren't these frameworks just for designers?"

Designers apply them in detail, but the PM owns the diagnosis and the success metric. Naming the problem type correctly is what keeps the team building the right thing.

"Isn't the Hook Model manipulative?"

It can be. Use the ethics test in section 06: build hooks for habits users want (saving money, exercising, learning), not for compulsive use that serves only your metrics.

"Where does AI prototyping fit?"

AI makes hi-fi prototypes cheap, which is a risk for need problems (you may over-polish too early) and a big win for engagement problems (you can test realistic, data-driven experiences quickly).
